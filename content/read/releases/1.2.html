The monotony of the road broke when four armed men appeared near its edge, three stepping forward while a fourth lingered behind. Their leader raised a hand with something close to courtesy and said, “Morning, travelers,” but Tiny had already changed. The ease went out of him, his voice flattened and sharpened, and without looking away from the men he pointed and ordered, “Zhale, left side of the road. Xiu, ten paces forward. Alastra, behind me.” Zhale angled left, ready to cast or shift. XiuQing took the forward space with calm, settled weight. Alastra stepped behind Tiny, not hidden, but plainly protected. They moved immediately, their formation snapping into place with an ease that made the command itself seem almost secondary, and the men opposite them stopped to read what had happened. No one drew. No one shouted. The leader held their gaze for a moment, lowered his hand, and said, “...never mind. Road’s all yours.” He withdrew, and the others with him. Tiny watched until they were clear, then resumed walking. “Well. That saved time.” Alastra studied him for several steps before saying, “That was surprisingly efficient. And extremely correct from a military standpoint. Where on earth did you learn that?” Tiny said, “I read a lot,” and Zhale grinned. “I think that was one of the most satisfying fights I’ve ever had.” Tiny gave her a sidelong look. “There was no--” Zhale said, “Exactly.”

That night they made camp beside the road with clear sight-lines and enough shelter against the cold. Their fire was small and practical, built for heat rather than comfort, and after they had eaten, the first edge of reserve wore down enough for the quiet to feel companionable. Zhale settled near the fire, caught Tiny’s eye, and said, “And you?” He looked up from adjusting a strap. “Pardon?” “We didn’t get your story.” For the first time all day, he seemed faintly wrong-footed. “Ah.” The others looked over, and he said, “Well. Not much to tell. Orphan, street rat out of the Field Ward, pickpocket and thief. I’ve worked as a bounty hunter the last twenty-one years.” Alastra watched him thoughtfully. “Still a thief?” “Absolutely,” he said. “I work sometimes for the small collection of people trying to establish a Thieves’ Guild in Waterdeep.” After that, the conversation thinned again. Bedrolls were unrolled, gear was stowed, and Tiny read for a while from a book no one remarked upon. Zhale banked the fire, and after brief discussion the watch order settled into place: XiuQing first, Zhale second, Alastra third, and Tiny last. During Alastra’s watch, a fox darted into the edge of the firelight, paused long enough to meet her gaze, then slipped away between the trees. Alastra dropped her hand, fire guttering out, and exhaled softly. “...right.” The night settled again and did not break a second time.

Morning came cold. Alastra woke first and lay still briefly before shifting within her bedroll in one brief, odd motion that passed unnoticed. She sat up as though nothing had happened. XiuQing rose soon after and began his slow, deliberate martial arts forms, each movement unhurried enough to look almost casual until the balance beneath it made clear how much discipline held the shape. Alastra watched him and asked, “...what is that?” XiuQing answered, “A morning practice,” and from her blankets Zhale muttered, “Looks like dancing.” He smiled at that, not insulted, not distant, the expression quick and bright in the cold morning air. “It is not dancing.” Zhale pushed herself upright. “Is it a Kozakura/Wa thing?” The correction came with the same easy warmth. “No. It is a tradition from Shou Lung, but both nations practice it.” Zhale’s expression changed at once. “Oh. I’m sorry. I didn’t mean, I mean, I didn’t want to assume. I hope I didn’t offend you.” XiuQing looked genuinely surprised, then smiled again, softer this time. “You did not.” He drew one slow breath, settling back into the form as if the answer and the movement belonged to the same practice. “I am difficult to offend by accident.” She let out a breath. “Good.” Tiny watched with quiet amusement, then kicked dirt over the last coals. Zhale noticed Alastra’s hair and said, “Your hair’s brown.” Tiny glanced over. “Shorter yesterday. Red before that. We taking bets now?” XiuQing’s gaze lingered only briefly, thoughtful but untroubled. Alastra said, “I told you. It does that.” Tiny grunted and said, “Eat. We’ve got miles.” They did, and set out again.

The second day passed in long reaches of road broken by shorter stretches of talk. As the land grew more familiar beneath her eye, Zhale gradually and almost unconsciously became their guide, pointing out a creek and explaining that it would eventually run into the Willowdeep, marking a line of hills where storms usually broke, and later indicating that most traders coming north from Daggerford used this road, but they would be turning off shortly. The others listened or answered when conversation required it and fell silent when it did not. By the time they made their second camp among sparse trees, the journey felt less like four people traveling together for the first time and more like four people beginning, cautiously, to travel well. Nothing troubled the second night beyond wind in the branches and the cry of a distant owl.

The third morning came colder, frost silvering the ground and making every breath visible. Alastra stirred first again, her hair different once more: braided, darker, and neatly arranged. No one remarked on it. She shifted briefly within her blankets before rising without comment, while XiuQing had already begun his practice and Tiny stood near the edge of camp with the stillness of someone awake for some time. Then a decent-sized branch snapped above Tiny and dropped against the side of his head. He straightened. Zhale stared. “...your ear.” Tiny reached up, caught the loosened piece, paused, and exhaled. “...well.” He removed it fully, revealing the difference beneath. “I’m not actually a hill giant goliath,” he said. “I’m a cloud giant goliath.” There was no flourish to it. He simply knelt, pulled a small bundle and a tin from his pack, and set to work with the calm competence of long practice, applying adhesive, fitting the replacement, and blending pigment until the illusion returned seamless enough that the moment almost seemed reversible. Alastra watched with professional interest. “...that is extremely good work.” “Practice,” Tiny said. “You could probably get a job at the Lightsinger Theater doing that.” “Think they’re accepting apprentices?” Zhale remained fixed on the basic issue, crossed her arms, and asked, “So why don’t you just be a cloud giant?” Tiny stood, slung his pack into place, and answered, “Because this way is easier.” Then he added, “And I’d appreciate it if you three helped me keep it that way, yes?”

Late that morning, the four of them left the High Road for a narrower path that dodged around fields divided by fences and low stone lines, and Zhale led them toward Fox Gap without hesitation. Gerd’s Trees rose ahead as ancient growth amid cultivated land, with Mama Laran’s half-timbered, thatched cottage at the center of the grove. They arrived in early afternoon, and Mama Laran stepped out to meet them and said, “Well. You took your time.” Zhale crossed to embrace her at once. Mama Laran looked Zhale over and said, “You’re eating. Good.” Oren Mossheart stood behind Mama Laran, and at the sight of him Zhale grinned. “Oren!” She embraced him too and asked, “Passing through?” Oren said, “Something like that.” There was time only for a quick meal, a few exchanged words, and the purpose of the stop. When asked about the river, Mama Laran listened, shook her head, and said, “I wish I had something useful for you. But nothing new. Something’s wrong, certainly. You can feel it in the ground if you pay attention. But it isn’t broken quite yet.” Her answer confirmed the shape of the unease, though it offered little new information. They did not linger. They returned to the road with their purpose unchanged.

Willowdale lay low beside the Willowdeep, its fields pressed close to the river’s edge. The river moved with an unusual smoothness that unsettled Zhale before she could name why. The villagers spotted The Four immediately, alert but with hope worn thin by waiting. In the square two cloaked figures stood slightly apart from the others: one tall and thin with pale fingers curled around a staff, the other green-skinned and narrow-eyed. The taller figure murmured, “Adventurers,” and the green-skinned figure said, “Maybe useful.” Before questions could take shape, a barefoot boy skidded to a stop before The Four and said, “You’re the ones. You’re the help.” He pointed toward the river. “Farmer Haskel was eeling. The water just grabbed him. It’s pulling him under.” The Four moved immediately, without pause, exchange of glances, or hurried planning, following him between cottages and narrow plots toward the river. The water already sounded wrong, louder and more chaotic than it should. Shouts carried over it: “Over there!” “Careful, it’s angry!” “Someone grab him!” Downstream, Farmer Haskel thrashed in the current. Upstream, the river boiled and folded over itself in unnatural surges.

Haskel vanished beneath the surface as The Four reached the bank. XiuQing stepped into the violent current, braced, and caught Haskel’s arm when it broke the surface again. “Try to hold still,” he encouraged. Alastra moved beside the bank and sent a silvery disk out, knowing it would follow the slope of the riverbed rather than the water. It hovered low enough to put an edge within Haskel’s grasp. Haskel lunged and caught it with his other hand, nearly losing his grip when the river surged, but XiuQing forced his arm higher and kept him holding on. Tiny entered from the other side, wading into the freezing current up to his waist and muttering, “Fantastic,” before setting his weight beneath Haskel’s shoulders and growling, “Not today.” Alastra held position on the bank, watching the water rather than the drowning man. “Is this magical...?” She reached inward for an explanation of the stream’s actions and found nothing useful. “...hm. Nothing obvious.” By then Zhale had reached the bank, sensed the deeper disturbance upstream, and turned immediately away from the rescue to run toward the source.

She found it above the worst of the current: a chest-sized mass of layered mineral, black beneath the water and rust-red above, with the river breaking around it in both directions as though the stone itself had become an argument the current could not resolve. Below, Tiny pressed deeper to catch Haskel’s other arm and nearly lost his footing when the river bucked hard against him. In that same instant, a figure of blue light and spray rose beside them. A naiad surfaced half from the water, wavering between flesh and current, and with one strained gesture steadied the flow just enough for Tiny to recover and lock his stance. She slipped beneath the surface again after only a breath, but the moment was enough. XiuQing forced Haskel’s second arm upward, Alastra drew the floating disk back toward shore one careful foot at a time, and waiting hands dragged Haskel clear. The naiad rose again, grimacing and weak with effort. Alastra blurted, “My gods, are you all right?” The naiad said, “Fine,” then lifted one shaking hand upstream. “Get that out. The stone. Upstream.”

Zhale was already moving toward the stone. She threw her rope once and missed, threw again and failed again, then adjusted her aim, caught a tree branch on the little island beside the stone, and hauled the rope taut to make a path through the current. Tiny clambered briefly clear of the water and shouted toward the spirit, “Can we help you?” The naiad said with obvious effort, “I’m fine,” then, “Get that. Quickly.” XiuQing came upstream to join Zhale while Alastra dismissed the disk and followed the river line with sharpening focus. “Naiads,” Alastra said. “What do I know about naiads...?” She came up with absolutely nothing and let the thought go. Zhale worked along the rope until the current rose to her chest and the branch bent beneath her weight. Tiny studied the angle, prepared another rope, and threw it across while saying, “Catch, hook it around the rock.” The naiad surfaced again just long enough to warn, “Don’t let it attach itself.” From the far side, XiuQing called, “Alastra,” then said, “my quarterstaff.” Alastra threw it across, and XiuQing caught it cleanly.

The first attempt to move what turned out to be a Heartstone did nothing. Zhale had the rope set, Tiny had the line, and XiuQing had wedged the quarterstaff beneath the stone, but the Heartstone sat as if rooted in a deeper world. “Try it now!” Zhale called, lowering herself farther into the frigid current until it reached her chin; she forced both arms beneath the stone, Tiny hauled, XiuQing levered upward. The Heartstone did not move. From the bank, Alastra stared at it and tried again to name what she was seeing. “All right,” she muttered, then, “what are you exactly...” Once again her search found nothing useful. Zhale planted her feet as best she could against the riverbed and shouted, “Now!” This time all three committed fully. Tiny pulled hard enough to wrench his shoulders taut, XiuQing drove the quarterstaff upward with both hands, and Zhale heaved from below. The stone broke free of the bed with sucking violence and rose a full foot into motion. “Go help Zhale!” Alastra snapped. Tiny splashed over beside Zhale without hesitation. The final strain came ugly and uneven, with XiuQing lifting one side alone while Tiny and Zhale struggled with the other, but between the three of them they awkwardly dragged the Heartstone onto the muddy bank. The river’s fury slackened immediately. The current did not become gentle, but it became itself again, and the naiad let out a long, trembling breath. Alastra’s tension drained abruptly, and she said, “Dammit. I’m so sorry. I kept trying to think of something useful, but...”

With the immediate danger past, Zhale approached the naiad first and asked permission before offering aid. XiuQing followed and stopped with Zhale at a respectful distance. “May this word ease your wounds,” he said, and Zhale gave a brief invocation to Gaia. Their magic settled over the naiad in two quiet waves, steadying the flicker of her form. When asked, the naiad named herself: “Essaerae.” Alastra gathered her manners and answered in careful Fey, “Greetings, oh blessed one.” Essaerae declined further healing in favor of time and the slow recovery of the river itself, but when asked what had happened, she gave what she knew. Three men had placed the Heartstone. Two had commanded. One had labored. The men had spoken of “testing” and “experiments” and repeated that “he will be happy,” with more hope than certainty. The one who set the stone had feared what he was doing, though he did it anyway, and whatever power the men meant to use, they did not understand it cleanly. Essaerae explained that the object was the Heartstone of an earth elemental and should be returned to its plane lest it continue poisoning the balance of this one. Zhale listened, weighed that truth, and said with regret, “We can’t do that yet.” Essaerae inclined her head and said, “Someplace calm then,” and added, “and slightly fey-touched... might help it balance for a time.” Zhale considered briefly and said, “I know just the place.”

The Four left the river with respectful farewells and began the slower, uglier work of moving the Heartstone upstream to a sheltered grove tucked away from the river. Branches and rope became a drag-frame. XiuQing set the pace. Tiny put his weight beside XiuQing and bore the strain. Zhale chose the path and corrected it whenever the frame snagged or listed. Alastra helped where she could, and when they reached the grove and began to dig, she summoned an unseen servant to hold back loosened soil. Sidelong glances and mild amusement followed until she protested, “Hey. I’m trying to help,” then proved it by keeping the removed earth from collapsing back in. The grove lay hidden, its quiet just removed enough from the ordinary world to satisfy what Essaerae had asked for. Tiny drove his crowbar into the soil. XiuQing broke packed earth with hammer and chisel. Zhale took over in earnest, using Mold Earth to open the pit in steady increments until it was deep enough for the stone. Together, they lowered the Heartstone into place and sealed it beneath the ground. Zhale smoothed the surface and restored what she could, and XiuQing followed with Mending, repairing roots and closing the smallest wounds left by the work. When the pit had vanished back into the grove as nearly as they could manage, Zhale began a ritual invoking Gaia, and the air around them settled into deliberate stillness.

Movement broke along the riverbank beyond the grove’s stillness, and Tiny was the first to turn and understand what he was seeing. “That’s him, I think, the one Essaerae described,” he said, already pointing. “After him.” Merrac had a lead, but not much. Tiny managed to close the distance first through sheer commitment, driving through Merrac’s attempted turn and crashing into him hard enough to send both of them into a nearby tree. Merrac hit badly and tried to scramble clear, but Tiny dragged him upright by the front of his tunic before he could get his feet under him. XiuQing arrived a heartbeat later and took position without speaking, while Zhale moved to close the last angle of escape. She glanced at the tree, then at Tiny, and asked, “Hey. Is that tree okay?” By the time Merrac understood his situation, the circle had closed. Alastra approached last, unhurried, and hissed, “That’s enough. Stop.” Merrac’s imagined resistance collapsed under the pressure of The Four. “Don’t hurt me,” he blurted. “I give. I give.”

Merrac’s explanation came in frightened fragments. He had been paid twenty-five gold immediately and promised twenty-five more after delivery of a recording token. He had not made the plans; he had only carried what he was told to carry, gone where he was told to go, and retrieved the token after the river test was done. When it and the Heartstone had been originally placed, there had been two other people. Merrac had watched more than he understood: the Heartstone lowered into the river, the token set near the working, the two men speaking of “results” and “conditions” as if the land itself were something that could answer questions if injured carefully enough. The token was not his. It belonged to the test, or to whoever had ordered the test, and once the work was finished, his job was to take it away. He knew no names and was not meant to know more. Based on how the others spoke, he thought perhaps someone stood above them, but he had not seen or heard enough to say. What he did know was that he was meant to meet a man named Harlon Lest three days from now, at six o’clock, at the abbey ruins north-northwest of here. Merrac was supposed to bring the river token and hand it over in person. Harlon would have another token to retrieve at the ruins, and from there the chain would continue.
