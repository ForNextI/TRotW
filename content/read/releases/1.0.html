PROLOGUE

16 Hammer 1501 DR

The Wardens noticed at once that the scene ahead was trying very hard to look like an accident. Rain slicked the muddy road into a ribbon of black glass, and an overturned wagon blocked the way on the stone bridge, lantern light guttering inside the rain-dark canvas. At first glance, it offered a clean story: snapped axle, bad weather, missing horses, a traveler’s luck gone sour. But Perception was not sharper eyesight; it was the refusal to accept the obvious. The closer they came, the less the wreck agreed with itself. Why would this have happened here? Were those reins cut, not torn? Where were the horses? The driver? Then they saw a fresh scar marked roughly into the bridge stone: a crude circle diagonally bisected from upper right to lower left. That stopped the four of them, not because the mark was strange, but because it was almost theirs. They always left their sigil as notice that things had been righted, not as a warning. Now someone had copied it badly and carved it beside a crime, familiar enough to be dangerous and wrong enough to offend the eye. Whoever had staged this scene had not merely wanted the wagon found; they had wanted the blame to find the Wardens. That was not acceptable. From beneath the bridge came the sharp sound of metal scraping stone. No cry for help followed. No panic. No prayer. The sound stopped, and in the rain-thick silence afterward, the scene felt less abandoned than interrupted. The Wardens held still until the sound started up again, a purposeful grating rising from underneath the bridge’s arch. Zhale signed, “Troll?” and Alastra stifled a giggle. They were half a day west of Daggerford, where the worked farms thinned toward Gerd’s Trees, and the road still wore the habit of safety: traveled enough to expect company, lonely enough to make rescue uncertain.

Tharad, who looked like about half of a scrawny hill giant, all long limbs and improbable height, signed instructions quickly to the others, sending Alastra the wizard and XiuQing the monk to the wagon while he and Zhale went below. Though he excelled at stealth, he did not make it the center of their approach; Zhale, the druid, was no shadow in slick mud, and pretending otherwise would serve neither caution nor pride. With one hand over his shoulder, lightly holding his greatclub’s leather handwraps, he descended the broken slope toward the swollen stream; Zhale followed, blue-skinned and tusked, murmuring the quick shape of a spell as Mage Armor settled around her and rain hissed over stone and grass. Before either of them had a clear view beneath the bridge, Tharad called out, “Hey there, friend,” and then, “Need any help?” The scraping stopped again. For a moment there was only water, brown and fast below the span, and then a controlled, strained voice answered from closer than expected: “Don’t come any closer.” A kneeling, soaked figure edged into view near the bridge footing, one hand braced against the rock, an iron pry bar loose in the other, its wedge end ground bright from being sharpened against the bridge stones, fresh blood staining the rock near one knee. “I’m not robbing anyone,” the figure said, eyes moving past Tharad and Zhale to measure distance, cover, and escape. “And I’m not trapped. Just... fixing something that broke at the wrong time.” Then his gaze flicked upward and his voice tightened. “If you’re the ones who left the mark up there, then we don’t need to be enemies.”

The man understood their silence before anyone corrected him. “...you didn’t,” he said, almost to himself. “Damn. Thought I was lucky for once.” He glanced at the pry bar. “Just making something to defend myself.” He shifted weight off the injured knee and winced. “I seen the symbol before. Other roads. Other places. This one just showed up today, after the wagon went over.” Tharad watched him, trying to decide whether they had caught the man seconds too late or only caught him in the wrong place under the wrong sign. The pry bar was wet, with a bright edge where it had been worked against the stone. The blood at the knee was real, and the tracks where he had limped down were still obvious despite the weather. The man’s tension did not feel rehearsed, and his breathing belonged more to pain than to the bright rhythm of a prepared lie, but someone had taken time to carve their mark, and Tharad did not have the luxury of assuming. When Zhale asked, “Are you badly hurt?” he answered readily, which told its own small truth. “Not crippling,” he said, then, “Just... stupid.” A little later, tighter: “Hurts. Won’t bleed out, I don’t think.”

Above, the Wardens’ signals kept the wagon’s answers from carrying down beneath the bridge. They all knew Common Sign Language, though XiuQing was still learning. Any time they split the party, sign was assumed unless speech was safe, necessary, or meant to be heard. Alastra’s findings moved down the slope: no bodies, no blood trail, no signs of struggle, crates opened selectively rather than smashed. XiuQing reported that the traces had been cut cleanly, probably not in panic, and that whoever took the heavier crate had known which one mattered. The injured man could see information moving without hearing it, and that unsettled him more than whispers would have. “I was under here when the wagon went,” he said. “Didn’t touch the horses. Didn’t touch the grain. Whatever you think you’re seeing, it wasn’t clean.” His grip on the pry bar loosened. “You came down here instead of drawing steel. That tells me something.” After another breath, he added, “I stayed because I figured if someone came, they’d look before they cut. And because whoever carved that mark wanted someone else to take the blame.” The pry bar finally dropped onto the wet stone.

Tharad signaled again, sending Zhale back uphill to join XiuQing. The monk was plainly from Kara-Tur, but his warm half-smile made him feel less distant than the man expected. Now that the stranger’s pry bar was down, Tharad let his own hand ease from the greatclub without surrendering the fact of it. Alastra came down to the footing without rushing. He signed to her, “Probably lying. Find out who framed us and why.” The man looked to Tharad first, then back to her, and something in his face shifted as he took her in: the violet bob of rain-dark hair, the elegant sweep of her copper horns, the polished ease that made beauty look almost like rank. Tiefling, maybe. Dangerous, definitely. “Hey there,” she said warmly. “What’s your name?” The man relaxed a fraction. “...Lon,” he said. “Lon Danner.” He gave a humorless little gesture toward his knee. “And before you ask, no, I wasn’t meant to be here when it went wrong.” Above them, Zhale and XiuQing found the truth the rain had not yet erased: purposeful tracks, burdened and calm, moving toward the riverbank. Lon’s shoulders dropped as he crossed from bracing for a fight into gambling on a conversation.

Alastra gave him a warm smile. “Hey, Lon, it’s real nice to meet you. We are going to help you get that crate back, and of course we want to know who took it and why, but first, I gotta say I don’t like the look of that knee. Mind if I cast a spell? This will help us sort out whether helping you is going to get anyone killed.” Lon let out a breath that was half laugh, half wince. “A spell?” he said, noticing that she had not knelt or reached for the wound. “If it keeps me walking, I won’t complain.” He did not step back. He did not guard himself. Whatever he expected, it was not intrusion. She turned back to the man, murmured in Draconic, and moved her hands to cast Detect Thoughts. At first she caught only surface scramble: damn thing’s swelling, don’t look at the horns, gods, that one’s tall, skinny though, don’t look afraid, I’m not afraid, gods, my knee, Elth’s fault, always too hard with the hand, the bridge stone, the mark cut wrong, too shallow, should have had chalk, no time, not enough time, the crate with the ledger, no, don’t let them know about that, keep her calm, pretty thing, damn knee, walking away means living, these are them Wardens, right? They mad about their mark? They figure out I carved it? Maybe still walking away, maybe... Lon watched her face. “Is... is that working?” he asked, less certain now.

Alastra pushed deeper, and Lon failed to keep her out. The clutter did not vanish; it organized around fear. No idea what the ledger holds. Just which crate. Move it, get paid, leave. gods, she's pretty, I'd like to bed her. These adventurers, these Wardens, I think they don’t kill, right? Elth Fenwick, arsehole, old and sharp-eyed, dresses like a toff, just arranges the thefts and never dirties his own hands. Slow the wagon, don’t overturn it, idiot. The broken axle had been a mistake. knee, stupid mistake. Elth is a mistake wearing a fancy coat. Godsdamn ledger. That ledger is proof. Bribes in cipher, names of minor lords, guild factors, rivers, payments marked delivered that never were, wrongdoing written down, enough to kill over. If these damn Wardens recover it, Elth will kill me. If Elth keeps it, someone worse will... As the deeper probe became unmistakable, Lon stiffened. “...no,” he said, “No, that’s not healing.” The river roared over stone as if trying to drown him. “You’re in my head. Godsdammit.” He did not bolt, and he did not reach for the pry bar. He looked at Alastra, then past her to Tharad and the empty slope, and the lie he had been building died before it reached his mouth. “...okay. Fine. If you’re going to turn me in, do it fast. If you’re going to help me... then we don’t dance around it anymore.” He met her eyes again. “You know about the ledger? And you know about Elth?” Alastra nodded. Then he gestured upriver. “Did you hear where they’re taking it? In my head? No? They’re not taking it by ground. They’re taking it by water.”

The warmth left Alastra’s face, and behind her Tharad rose to his full seven feet and seven inches, one hand resting again on the greatclub. “Honestly, Mr. Danner,” Alastra spat in a dangerous whisper, “I don’t like you, or your tiny little mind. Lead us, and we better get to that ledger before it gets wet, understand? Now move!” Her Intimidation worked. Tharad’s voice cut over the water. “Wardens, we’re rolling,” as Alastra jerked on Lon’s arm, telling him to get up. Lon went pale with the internal collapse of a man discovering that negotiation had ended several breaths ago. “Okay. Okay. Yes.” His injured knee nearly failed him, but fear got him up and moving. “Boat,” he blurted. “Flat-bottom skiff. Tied off downstream, under them reeds. Elth didn’t want it seen from the bridge. They’ll be loading already. Once it’s on water, they won’t stop for anything.” Above, Zhale and XiuQing found the tracks veering hard toward the riverbank, the churn of shifted weight and the faint drag of a hull through wet grass. Zhale pressed something into XiuQing’s hand before they moved. “Ledger’s wrapped,” Lon added, desperate. “Waxed leather. It’ll survive a dunk, but not long.” He limped forward. “This way. Thirty seconds, maybe less.”

They had talked too long, and Tharad felt it in the marrow. He took Lon’s arm from Alastra and broke into a controlled run, half-dragging the injured man; not a blind sprint, but the pace of someone who expected danger to object from any direction. He looked back once and saw the others coming. Then XiuQing passed him, not loudly or messily, but suddenly, feet barely troubling mud and grass as he moved at full speed toward the river. Through the reeds, the skiff came into view, half-hidden and already sliding away from the bank as weight shifted aboard. One man braced with a pole. Another hauled a heavy, oil-darkened crate toward the center. Lon shouted, “Elth!” and then, “Help!” Elth looked up, saw the Wardens, calculated, and snapped, “Leave him. Push off!” The pole dug into the riverbed, and the skiff began to slide free.

XiuQing launched himself from the bank, doing a swift flip in midair to get his feet under him, and landed hard in the center of the skiff between the three men, forcing space where there had been none. The boat bucked, water slapping over the low gunwale; the pole man staggered and nearly lost the pole, while the crate-hauling man yelped and lurched back. “Hells!” Elth shouted, scrambling for balance. XiuQing did not try to take the pole. Instead, he grabbed the man’s hands and used them to jam it down and back, forcing it to bite mud, using the pole man’s panic against him. The skiff slewed sideways toward the bank, its escape line broken, as the poler cried out from the wrench in his shoulder. Tharad shoved Lon toward Alastra and said, “Hold on to that,” then used his thief-trained body to make the sufficient but awkward jump: angle, distance, timing, greatclub still with him. He hit the skiff hard enough to make the wood groan, turning the crowded boat into a problem attempting to remain afloat. Seeing that Alastra had control of Lon, Zhale changed into wolf form and launched herself for the prow, crashing into the front man with wet snarling weight and driving him back until the crate shifted dangerously. On the bank, Alastra held Lon in one hand and let fire coil in the other. She did not cast. Everyone aboard only needed to see that she could.

The skiff slammed sideways into the muddy bank, half-grounded, half-afloat. Elth’s hands came up. “Don’t kill me! Gods, don’t...” XiuQing threw his backpack ashore and moved with the boat rather than against it, letting one foot slip into the river so his weight could counter the roll. The hull steadied, low and awkward, but no longer trying to drown anyone. Zhale set her teeth against the front man’s throat without piercing, pinning him with pressure enough to make the terms of survival obvious. Tharad spread his legs wide as ballast, dropped his own pack, pulled rope free with one hand, and hauled the pole man down with the other until resistance went out of him and the pole clattered loose. On the bank, Alastra spun Lon to face her, pushed on his shoulders, and commanded, “Sit.” He sat. She doused the fire and replaced it with a simple, elegant electrum dagger, lifting his chin with the point until he met her eyes. “And stay.” Lon swallowed slowly and obeyed. Elth, the only man still upright because no one had yet bothered to put him down, rasped, “Alright. Alright. You win.”

They dragged the skiff fully onto land and bound the four men so that they were seated back-to-back in a tight square: Elth, the pole man, the front man, and Lon. Wrists were tied behind them, ankles secured enough to keep them seated without cutting off circulation, and their gear was stripped and thrown beyond reach. The oilskin-wrapped, iron-banded crate they dragged off the skiff and set on solid ground, heavy and purposeful. Tharad gave Alastra a quick nod. Zhale shifted back from wolf form, and XiuQing stepped from the shallows with water streaming down his leg as Alastra came forward with the electrum dagger still present, not raised, merely there. “Alright, gentlemen,” she said. “Where are you taking the ledger, and who is going to want to kill you for taking it?”

Elth answered immediately. “Downriver. South. There’s a waystation, old ferry stop, half collapsed. Locals call it Blackwake Bend. Boats can tie up there out of sight. From there it goes inland by cart.” His eyes flicked toward the crate. “To a buyer I never met. Not directly. Middlemen. Always middlemen.” The second question cost him more. “And who is gonna want us dead? ...everyone.” One of the other men gave a small, broken laugh, and Elth shot him a look before continuing because silence would be worse. “The ledger isn’t just corruption. It’s connective tissue. It ties rivers to guild factors. Guild factors to nobles. Nobles to mercenary contracts that never officially existed. If it surfaces, some people lose positions. If it’s read, some people lose heads.” He looked directly at Alastra. “House guards. Guild enforcers. Private knives that don’t wear colors. And if it reaches the wrong hands, people who don’t care about leverage, then everyone who ever touched it becomes a loose end. Which includes me. And them. And now... probably you.” One of the men shook and said, “Elth said it was just records. Just paper.” Elth snapped, “It is just paper! That’s the problem!” Then his shoulders sagged. “So we were taking it somewhere no one important could admit they owned it... and hoping to get paid before someone decided silence was cheaper.” He looked at the Wardens and could not stop himself. “...where are you taking it?”

Alastra slapped him hard enough to make the rain-bright air ring. It was not wild or emotional, but clean, practiced, and final. “You don’t get to ask questions.” She tilted his chin up with the dagger. “You don’t get to decide what matters. And you certainly don’t get to pretend this is some clever game of leverage. You’re going to answer what I ask. You’re going to answer it cleanly. And then you’re going to sit very still while we decide whether you live long enough to regret your career choices.” Elth swallowed. Alastra did not raise her voice. “Start over. Who wants this ledger most, and who is closest?” Then she spun the knife through her fingers like a carnival trickster and said, “See, I have this spell. It’s called Mind Spike, and I could literally drive this psionic wedge deep into your brain. If it doesn’t kill you, which it probably will, I could track you to the furthest corners of Faerûn.” She drew a little copper wire flower from her pouch and began the motions of a spell before pausing deliberately. “But I’m not going to cast that right now.”

Her verbal components slipped into Draconic, a personal habit of casting rather than a separate kind of magic, and Detect Thoughts opened Elth at the surface instead. His mind tried to become a locked room and failed: old river warehouse, half-submerged pilings, blue-black wax, don’t think the name, don’t think cutter, don’t think burn the trail, a woman’s calm, bored voice saying, If it moves, you burn the trail. His fear was not street fear or guild fear, but institutional dread, the terror of signatures, ledgers, sealed contracts, and men who vanished without leaving a scandal behind. She’s not bluffing, she knows Mind Spike, she’s not angry, that’s worse. The name kept surfacing no matter how hard he tried to bury it. Vellisar. Alastra realized they were close, too close. The cutter was already barely discernible upriver. Elth whispered, “You said you weren’t going to cast it.” She had not, not Mind Spike. She had only let fear do the work, and that prompted Elth into speaking. “Vellisar Consortium. River contracts. Shipping insurance. Dispute resolution.” A bitter edge broke through. “That last one’s a joke.” He looked toward the dark water. “They don’t want the ledger destroyed. They want it owned. Controlled. Used.” Behind them, Tharad heard it now too: oars cutting through water upstream.

Between Elth’s words and his unguarded fear, Alastra understood the ledger was not originally Vellisar’s. It belonged to a third party, and the consortium had acquired or intercepted it to monopolize leverage, not to destroy it. “Who does the ledger belong to?” she asked. Tharad’s warning came low beside her. “Alastra, we’re going to need to move soon.” This time Elth answered cleanly. “It belonged to Aldren Vos. Factor for three inland houses. Vos kept records as insurance. Everyone knew it. Bribes, favors, shipping irregularities, quiet violence, all written down, cross-referenced. He thought paper made him safe. Someone tipped Vellisar that the ledger was moving. They intercepted it before Vos could decide where to hide it next.” Then, colder: “Vos is dead. Has been for two tendays. ‘River accident.’” The Wardens knew enough for the names to matter. Vos had been a meticulous, paranoid noble factor whose death had been quietly noted in the press, and by river factions and guild auditors, without producing a real inquiry, which implied someone had made sure no inquiry would grow teeth. Vellisar’s daylight face was shipping insurance, river arbitration, and contract enforcement; beneath that lay economic pressure, quiet removals, and ownership through obligation. Alastra knew the Force Grey warning: If a guild disappears without scandal, check Vellisar’s books. Tharad knew the underworld version: Vellisar never touched anything unless it was already valuable, and never moved personally unless the stakes were high and the risk low. A cutter coming now meant the ledger was no longer dormant.

Tharad made the decision in sign, sharp and fast: “You two get going. We’ll catch up with you at King’s Cutoff.” Zhale and Alastra vanished toward the fallback spot without hesitation. For one breath, only Tharad and XiuQing remained with the bound men, the crate, and the ledger still hidden inside it. Tharad turned to XiuQing and signed, “Get that ledger open and ruffle through pages. Make it look like it was read, then dump it between the backs of these idiots.” Then he was gone after the others, long strides eating wet ground. XiuQing moved at once, using the blacksmith’s nippers Zhale had pressed into his hand, cutting the crate’s iron bands, ripping it open, and drawing out the thick damp ledger while staying low. He flipped through pages messily, bent corners, smeared mud across sheets and columns. Then he quickly rewrapped it in the oilcloth and jammed it between the four bound men before sprinting away low and fast, grabbing his backpack as he ran.

By the time Elth understood the trick, XiuQing was gone. The cutter’s oars were close enough for the prisoners to hear, and panic bloomed in their eyes as they felt the ledger lying uselessly between their backs. Vellisar’s people would find four terrified men and no Wardens in sight. The scene would make no clean sense, which was exactly its purpose. The Wardens vanished into the trees as rain swallowed their tracks and the branches closed behind them, slowing only once the river had faded and the undergrowth thickened. Zhale angled them away from the familiar lines of the land, turning them from any path that might lead back toward Mama Laran’s. Many in Willowdale knew her, knew the ties that bound her to that place, and none of them would risk drawing danger there. The others followed without question, the decision understood before it was spoken. At King’s Cutoff, in the shadow of a copse where the ground hardened and the undergrowth thinned, they regrouped, adjusting cloaks, settling packs, wiping mud from their hands as the last edge of urgency gave way to clarity. Quiet confirmations passed between them, no louder than breath: Brindlewash was out, Eelbrook was out, there would be no doubling back and no clever shortcuts. They chose the most unremarkable path they could find, the main public road, where traffic and noise and witnesses would serve as cover, and where Waterdeep loomed not only as a destination but as camouflage. By the time they stepped onto the packed earth, they looked like any other travelers, moving with purpose rather than haste, indistinguishable in the slow tide of the road. Behind them, near the bend of the river, Vellisar’s people would find only fragments of a broken design: a ruined plan, terrified men, and a ledger that had been read just long enough to matter.
