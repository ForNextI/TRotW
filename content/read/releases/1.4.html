Four poorly equipped, desperate bandits came late the next day. Alastra raised a hand and called, “Gentlemen,” then added, “I strongly advise you reconsider.” They did not, and she sighed. “Very well.” The fight ended quickly under the combined force of The Four. Afterward, Alastra stepped forward and said, “Now,” then, “Let’s try this conversation a second time.” The bandits surrendered. Tiny waved them off with, “Find a better profession,” adding, “And stop robbing travelers,” and they were allowed to leave minus their weapons, which were broken and then buried a ways away.

Mama Laran’s voice reached them again as they traveled, carrying news collated by her local group of hedge druid friends: three new incursion sites had been confirmed elsewhere in the Vale. The message arrived not as a letter or ordinary voice but as a storm of wings and sound. Sparrows descended in a sudden cloud around Zhale, darting and weaving while the air filled with overlapping chatter. Each bird tried to speak at once, their voices tumbling over one another with urgency but no clarity. Zhale, trying to make sense of them with Speak with Animals, raised both hands. “Calm down. One at a time.” The birds did not listen. Their voices pressed forward, insistent, and meaning slipped through the gaps. Alastra stepped closer and cast Tongues, clearly expecting the spell to catch a language where Zhale’s expression had found only crisis and feathers. For one bright, hopeful breath, she listened with magical confidence. Then nothing useful happened. “Oh,” she said. “Apparently that is not how sparrows work.” Zhale gave her a look from inside the storm of wings. “Helpful.” “I attempted culture,” Alastra said, and stepped back with dignity, leaving Zhale to gather the message bit by bit from the noise until the confirmed names emerged: there were other sites: Quiet Lake, Silent Patch, and Ashwater Ford. The Four further gleaned that these places were also Old Places, millennia old, but they had been strange for generations, perhaps two centuries or more, and that the new strangeness had endured without escalation until recently. They absorbed this bundle of facts pieced together from the confused and excited flock, who were very proud of themselves, and continued on to Waterdeep, with the shape of the problem sharpening rather than resolving.

“So,” Tiny said, “based on what we talked about last night, and the answers your excited messengers just brought, we have what, three layers to this thing? Old Places, like really old, are affected, but not all, just a few certain ones. Those were noticed to have changed two, two and a half centuries ago. They became strange, but they didn’t act up until about two tendays ago. And then there’s a chain of people collecting tokens after somebody does something stupid at some of these sites?” Zhale answered, “Well, that’s complicated,” and XiuQing said, “Yes.” “So we’re interrupting somebody’s two-hundred-and-fifty-year-old plan? That’s usually not a good idea,” Alastra added. The chain now led unmistakably toward Waterdeep, toward a meeting meant for others before The Four intervened, and the meeting might no longer be what it had been when first arranged. Enough time had passed for word of Blackroot Ford to travel ahead of The Four, whether by people, magic, or the unseen structure holding the chain together, so the question was not simply what the meeting was but what it had become in their absence.

“Trap, trap, trap,” Zhale said. Alastra did not dismiss the possibility. “It is still the next link,” she said. “At Blackroot Ford, maybe we changed that, but probably not. Ressa and Calder are probably halfway to Chult by now.” Tiny said, “I doubt this obscured organization knows, but if they do, yes, they’ll change it. At that point it doesn’t matter what it was before.” “Come on,” Zhale said. “That is bait.” Alastra recalibrated. “Then we assume maybe it’s been turned,” she said. “We stop pretending it’s still the meeting it was supposed to be.” Tiny said, “If it’s set properly,” then added, “knowing it’s bait doesn’t stop the hook from working. It just means we see the line.” Zhale said, “I am serious. They are going to try to box us in.” Alastra countered, “Unless they do not know enough to change anything.” The Four fell quiet, not in disagreement but in recognition of the shape of the thing ahead. Each traced the matter from a different angle and arrived near the same conclusion without yet deciding what to do. The road carried them forward, and the decision was not yet made but no longer distant. XiuQing spoke then. “If it has been changed,” he said, “then it is something placed on our path. Our path is what we choose it to be.”

They held to their course and reached Waterdeep after dark, entering by the lesser known ways rather than through the main gates: culverts, service paths, and half-forgotten entries beneath and between the city’s outer edges. The road shifted subtly into the city beneath their feet, and Waterdeep received them without ceremony. Tiny led The Four through narrow streets and hidden turns until they reached a cramped squat tucked between larger buildings in the Southern Ward. He deactivated some wards, opened the door, and said, “Flat Four.” When asked why, he answered that it was the fourth place he had lived. Alastra sighed, “Sheesh, we need to come up with a real name for us. Now we really sound like a bad limerick or something. ‘The Four in Flat Four opened the door and slept on the floor.’” No one responded. Inside, the safe house was enough: quiet, hidden, spare, and for the moment safe. The others looked around the spartan room and accepted it without complaint. After about half an hour, Alastra rose as if remembering an appointment no one else needed to know about. “I’ll be back in a few hours,” she said, gathering her cloak. “Private business.” Tiny looked up just long enough to measure whether the words meant danger, then decided they did not. XiuQing accepted the boundary without comment. Zhale gave her the smallest amused glance and let it pass. Alastra smiled, not apologetically, and went out into Waterdeep alone.

The next morning, all four of The Four returned to the markets with the deliberate air of people who had decided that uncertainty was not an excuse to be underprepared. Waterdeep’s stalls had a way of making survival look almost casual: trays of rings beside boiled sweets, hedge-charms hanging from awning poles, knives laid out beside prayer beads, a woman selling weatherproof cloak buckles while arguing theology with a man whose cart smelled violently of fried onions. They moved through it slowly, not buying much, not wasting coin, each of them drawn at last to one small thing that felt less like equipment than a question placed in the hand. XiuQing chose a plain, weighty thumb band called the Iron Ring of Resolve, unlovely and nearly invisible once worn, but warm at moments when hesitation might cost action, and once each day able to sharpen his attention into a clear, steady line. Zhale chose the Wildwood Band, vine and leaf twined into a ring that seemed almost grown rather than worked, a charm that deepened her sense of living things, could call roots and growth to hold ground for a little while, and lent a stubborn resistance against being caught or bound by the world’s green hands. Tiny took a Worry Stone, nothing more elaborate than a smooth river-stone worn by the shape of decades of thumbs, though the vendor insisted it could break the first clutch of fear or influence if gripped hard enough, and that every use brought up some remembered moment when its bearer had already chosen not to yield. Alastra purchased the Vellum of Dawn, a folded scrap that would reveal one word each morning before fading, suggestion rather than command, but the kind of small omen she could imagine using, arguing with, or pretending not to follow. Coin changed hands, pouches closed, and by the time they returned to Flat Four each of them carried a tool, yes, but also a private little hinge on which some future choice might turn.

That afternoon Tiny went alone to the Finders’ Guild, leaving the others in the cramped quiet of Flat Four with the meeting in Waterdeep, the tokens, and the shape of the unseen chain spread among them as if the room itself had become a table for things not yet understood. Without Tiny’s voice, the conversation lost its thief’s angle and approached the problem from other doors. Alastra named the scale first, the possibility that whatever they had stumbled into already reached from the Vale into Waterdeep through people, ledgers, arranged meetings, and enough discipline to keep every courier ignorant of the whole design. Zhale kept pulling them back to the places themselves, because to her Quiet Lake, Silent Patch, and Ashwater Ford were not abstractions, not rooms full of men with hidden names, not schemes someone else might be baiting or turning, but hurt ground and wounded water within reach of ordinary people. XiuQing listened more than he spoke, folding each conclusion into the next without forcing one into dominance. By the time the talk thinned and no answer had been declared, the answer had nevertheless begun to root among them: the meeting mattered, the chain mattered, but letting the chain choose the next step might already be surrender of a kind.

The Finders’ Guild hall received Tiny with the particular ease of a place that knew him without ever needing to claim him. Kestrel Vane stood behind the desk, reading one sheet while keeping a finger on another; Brannik “Hooks” Feld leaned over a map with the tip of a knife placed beside a route; Maven sorted slips into small stacks whose logic no outsider would have guessed and no guild member would have questioned. Hooks looked up first. “Well now. Look what the tide dragged back.” “Morning,” Tiny said, and the word carried enough history to be greeting, report, and apology for absence all at once. Kestrel glanced up, said, “Tiny,” and slid a parchment toward him with no ceremony. “We’ve got something that might suit you.” He saw the name written there and felt recognition click into place before the rest of the job had even taken shape. “Oh.” Hooks lifted an eyebrow. “You know him?” “Yeah,” Tiny said, tapping the parchment once. “I actually know that guy.” “Good or bad?” Kestrel asked. Tiny considered. “I’ve got a strong feeling I know exactly where he’ll be.” Hooks smiled without warmth. “Well, that saves me the trouble of explaining his habits.” Kestrel named the fee, fifty gold, and Tiny echoed it only because fifty gold for fetching a man from his favorite failure was the sort of generosity that meant someone else was tired of waiting. He accepted, tucked the parchment away, and when Hooks called, “Back before lunch?” he answered, “Probably,” because some men made being found into a profession and some made being missing into a poorly maintained hobby.

The Sleeping Snake was already loud when Tiny arrived, though it was not yet highsun. Dockworkers crowded the place shoulder to shoulder, men and women alike shouting over mugs, dice, and one another, the air thick with old ale, wet wool, frying fat, and the particular joy of people whose labor had given them an appetite strong enough to forgive almost any room. At the far end, a little platform held two dancers: a tall woman whose bracelets flashed in sharp arcs and a lean young man whose steps glided and cut across the boards while a table of female dockhands roared approval. One of them slammed her mug hard enough to spill foam and shouted, “Spin him again!” Tiny took all this in and gave it none of his time. He found Pelder Wick exactly where he expected him, seated with a woman in a green sash, halfway through a story that had already wandered away from its own point. Tiny placed one hand on Pelder’s shoulder. “Morning.” Pelder turned, recognized him slowly, and said, “Oh,” with the weary dignity of a man discovering that consequences had learned to walk. Tiny tipped his head toward the door. “Time to go.” The woman in the green sash smirked. “Debt hearing?” Pelder rose. “...probably.”

They had nearly reached the door when a hand caught Tiny’s elbow, light and sure. The lean dancer stood there, breath steady despite the speed of the music still pulling at the stage behind him. “You’re Tiny, right?” Tiny nodded. The dancer gestured toward Pelder. “What’s his name?” “Pelder Wick.” “Thought so.” A gold coin flipped through the smoky air, and Tiny caught it without needing to look down. “Thanks,” the dancer said, already turning back toward the boards as the music resumed and the dockhands shouted for him again. Behind the bar, Alard Belaerl lifted his gaze long enough to meet Tiny’s eyes, and the nod between them was brief, practiced, and sufficient. Then Tiny stepped into the Dock Ward morning with Pelder in tow, the coin disappearing into his hand and the Snake swallowing the little disturbance behind them.

He returned quickly enough to annoy professional standards. Hooks looked up, began, “Well I’ll be,” then checked the light through the front windows and revised himself. “...that was barely an hour.” Tiny nudged Pelder forward. “Told you I knew where he’d be.” Kestrel counted out fifty gold with the clean economy of a woman who believed money became most useful when it moved to the right hand quickly, and as Tiny reached for it the world tightened around a single point inside his skull. A voice came without sound, precise and private, delivering twenty-five words in the old manner, without greeting, explanation, or room for misunderstanding. Tiny went still for less than a breath, then answered, “M.” Hooks blinked. “...what?” “Nothing.” Kestrel watched him more carefully. “Everything normal?” “Yep,” Tiny said, pocketed the gold, and left before any of them could make curiosity into an obligation.

Back at Flat Four, he set the coins on the table with a clink heavy enough to argue for itself. Zhale looked at the money and said, “...that was fast.” “Finders’ Guild.” Alastra considered him across the table. “That seems like an absurd amount of money for an hour’s work.” “I knew where he was.” XiuQing said, “Efficient.” Tiny nodded, then added, as if mentioning weather, “I’ve got another job tomorrow.” Zhale looked up. “You just finished one.” “Thieves’ Guild.” That shifted the room more than he had expected, or perhaps exactly as much. “You’re going to do a Thieves’ Guild job?” Zhale asked. “Yeah.” Alastra’s eyes narrowed slightly. “...you work for the Finders’ Guild and the Thieves’ Guild?” Tiny gave her the mild look of a man being asked whether doors could open both ways. “I told you: I do little jobs sometimes. Wanna help?” Zhale asked why they would want any part of that, XiuQing asked what help would mean, and Tiny explained that the job would be safer with lookouts. When Alastra asked why they would agree, Tiny tapped the gold on the table. “Because it pays.” Zhale said, “Done.” Alastra said, “Done.” XiuQing inclined his head. “Very well.” “Good,” Tiny said, and that was the meeting.

Only after that did they return, with Tiny present, to the other decision they had been circling since the Vale. The mysterious appointment in Waterdeep remained what it had been, or perhaps had already stopped being that the moment they interfered at Blackroot Ford. It might still be the next link in the chain; it might be an empty room; it might be a test, a trap, a warning, or nothing more than a courier’s expectation. They laid the matter out plainly and followed it until all the glamour of the next clue fell away. Behind the chain seemed to stand an organization that could create very unorthodox magic and plan centuries ahead; stepping into that mechanism now might mean entering a conspiracy with more hands, more years, and more rooms than they could master. The Vale, by contrast, had already spoken in three hurt places, and three more waited: Quiet Lake, Silent Patch, Ashwater Ford, and whatever else Mama Laran’s people might yet name. It seemed those places would simply worsen. In the end, the decision did not feel brave or cautious so much as properly aimed: they would not attend the meeting. They would return to Fox Gap and continue the work they had begun, helping in overt, real ways, helping things that did not seem able to help themselves. Taking on secret cabals might be possible when they were more experienced and more powerful. First, though, because rent and roads and future trouble all demanded coin, they would do Tiny’s job.

They spent the following day preparing with the care of people who meant to make a crime look smaller than it was. The target warehouse sat in a part of Waterdeep that smelled of rope, damp crates, old river trade, and private ledgers no one displayed in daylight. They watched it from several angles, never too long from one place, reading patterns instead of walls: who came in with keys, who left with ink on their fingers, which windows were ignored, which door received habitual glances from bored men pretending not to guard it. Roles settled without argument. XiuQing and Alastra would remain near the entrance as healer and wounded patient, their presence explained by posture and proximity before anyone thought to ask; Zhale would take the far side in wolf form, all nose, ear, and shadow, ready to sound warning if the street changed; Tiny would enter alone, because the job was built around a single hand doing one quiet thing very quickly. By night the street had emptied into a kind of workaday stillness, not deserted enough to be theatrical, not busy enough to be safe. XiuQing leaned close to Alastra, who sat with her back against a wall, and murmured with the steady intimacy of someone concerned over a patient, while she held herself in composed discomfort so convincingly that a passing laborer glanced once and decided not to inherit the problem. Zhale vanished into the dark between buildings. Tiny headed to the proper building, timing his movements to the flow of the neighborhood.
