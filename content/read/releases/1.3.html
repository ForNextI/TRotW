The Four judged Merrac quickly and chose restraint. XiuQing watched him briefly and said, “Go.” Tiny pressed the remaining amount of coin Merrac was owed into his hand with firm deliberation. “Take it,” he said, then warned him, “And get your family out of here. If whoever’s behind this decides to clean up loose ends...” He did not finish because he did not need to. Merrac nodded rapidly and said, “I will. I swear it.” He turned and fled without looking back. XiuQing followed him with his eyes for a moment before lowering his gaze. The choice settled quietly among The Four, and no one argued with it or embellished it. They turned together and took the road again, setting their course north-northwest toward the ruins. Their plan was to push hard, march two full days, and close the final distance in half a day. They held to it without deviation, walking until light failed and finding a place to settle beneath sparse trees.

Zhale’s hand rose without warning and held them in place. A sharp, unmistakable smell reached them a breath later as a small black-and-white creature waddled into the path ahead with its tail already half-raised. Tiny leaned to see past XiuQing and asked, “That a threat?” Zhale said, “Yes,” and The Four held their ground as the creature considered them and passed unhurried into the brush. Tiny waved his hand in front of his nose as he let out a slow breath and said, “Noted.” Their watch that night was in the now familiar order: XiuQing, Zhale, Alastra, Tiny. Zhale sent a brief magical message back to Mama Laran: “We’re following a chain tied to several strange sites. We’ll check in again if we return near Fox Gap.”

Two days of hard pace brought The Four within reach of the ruins. Morning arrived quiet rather than empty, and XiuQing rose and began his practice, each motion grounded in breath and intention. Zhale joined him without a word and mirrored the sequence carefully. Tiny joined a moment later, and the three moved in shared rhythm that required no instruction or correction. Alastra stirred behind them and sat upright. Tiny lowered his hands and stared. “...What happened to your hair?” Zhale asked. “...Did it get longer overnight?” Alastra lifted a blond strand, considered it, and answered, “...Yes.” Tiny rubbed his face once and said, “...Of course it did.” No one pressed further.

By late afternoon, the abbey lay ahead as broken stone and collapsed walls, worn more by abandonment than by time. The Four searched carefully without discussion, not to disturb the site but to understand it: fallen slabs, old wall-lines, pockets of loose soil, narrow hollows where something small could have been hidden by a person who knew the place. There were too many possibilities and no reason to guess badly. Merrac’s token stayed with them, wrapped and secured, while they withdrew into concealment and let the ruins keep their own secret until the appointed hour. At precisely six, a cautious, purposeful man approached. He did not wander. He counted stones beneath his breath, checked the angle of a fallen wall against the sinking light, and moved to a place The Four had not marked as important: a half-buried lintel tilted beneath thorn and old moss. There he knelt, scraped away loose grit with two fingers, and drew a small token from a narrow gap in the stone. He held it only long enough to confirm it, then closed his hand around it and looked across the ruins. “Merrac?”

No answer came. Harlon Lest waited, listening to the wind move through broken stone, and the silence changed him by degrees. “Merrac?” he called again, quieter this time, less command than question. His gaze moved over the ruins, realizing the person expected to meet him was not there. He tucked the recovered token away, turned once toward the path, and put one hand near his belt. Whatever he had been told to expect, this was not it. The Four moved before Harlon could choose whether to run. Alastra struck first, Zhale’s magic cut low and fast, Tiny moved inside Harlon’s space to break balance and drive him down, and XiuQing closed the remaining opening. The exchange ended almost immediately. “I’m not talking!” Harlon blurted, his resistance collapsing even as he spoke. Tiny took the ruins token from him and held it beside Merrac’s, the two small pieces suddenly heavier than their size allowed. The structure emerged in fragments: no names, only middlemen, meet, confirm, carry forward, and the next place and time always provided but never explained. Harlon had been sent to collect the token left at the ruins and receive Merrac’s token from the river test. After that, he was to carry both onward to the next handoff. Tiny asked, “Forward to who?” Harlon said, “No names,” then added, “Just the next step. The two in charge of this area.” Zhale asked, “And they keep the chain going?” and Harlon answered, “Yes.” XiuQing watched him a moment longer, then said, “You are not the source of this. Go.”

Tiny pressed coin into Harlon’s hand and said, “The rest of your pay,” and Harlon’s confusion flickered into understanding as Tiny continued, “We did the same for Merrac. You’re done. With this. With them. You go somewhere else.” Tiny asked, “You’re from Waterdeep, right?” Harlon nodded, and Tiny said, “Take your family and get clear.” Alastra stepped closer and said, “We don’t want you to pay for our interference.” Zhale said, “Nice meeting you,” then looked embarrassed. Harlon nodded once, then again, and left quickly, though not running. The space he had occupied lingered briefly before releasing. Tiny shook the tokens in his hand like dice and said, “That’s two,” and XiuQing replied, “And the pattern holds,” while Zhale looked north. “Three days. Blackroot Ford.” Alastra said, “And two waiting for us there.”

The Four made camp among the ruins, the fire small and tucked low between stones as conversation turned quietly practical. Zhale watched the coals and said, “If we keep paying everyone we help,” then continued, “we’re going to be broke before we make it halfway through whatever this is.” Tiny snorted. “Then we start charging people.” Zhale said, “That’s not what I mean.” Alastra said, “You’re not wrong.” Tiny poked at the fire. “We’re not trying to win the road. We’re trying to follow the chain. But yeah... next time someone points a knife at me, I might keep it.” XiuQing said, “Mercy does not require forgetfulness.” The rest looked at him in their XiuQing is being deep again way. The fire burned low, the watch passed, and the sky remained clear.

They broke camp at first light and resumed the road toward Blackroot Ford. The land grew quieter as they left the traveled routes behind until the quiet became a condition rather than an absence. The quiet broke as a shift rather than a surprise: first a low, wrong sound threaded with hunger, then movement as six gnolls peeled free from the brush ahead. Two took the path while the others fanned wide with practiced instinct to close the space around The Four. One said, “We’ll take your stuff,” then, “And have our way with your dead bodies while they’re still warm.” Tiny’s gaze moved to each flank. “Alright,” he said. “That’s clear.” The attackers came all at once. Tiny’s dart struck first as he stepped through the nearest attacker’s space and then brought the greatclub down. XiuQing redirected rather than resisted, turning the press of bodies back on itself, while Alastra’s fire broke the attackers’ cohesion at the point of contact and Zhale, already in wolf form, closed the flank. The fight became less a battle than a collapse. The attackers pressed harder at the first sign of injury and lost what little advantage they had as discipline broke into frenzy. The clearing fell still as abruptly as it had broken. Zhale looked down at the fallen shapes, expression unreadable. Tiny said, “Well... not everything gets to walk away,” and XiuQing replied, “Evil should not.” They buried the bodies and had a quick toast to their fallen foes.

By the third day, the sound of broader, slower water returned ahead. Blackroot Ford revealed itself in pieces, the crossing spreading thin across worn stone in a sheet too even to trust. The quiet of the place felt arranged rather than grown. The Four separated along the lines Tiny indicated. The thief slipped low along the near bank while XiuQing, Zhale, and Alastra took the rise beyond the crossing and settled into concealment. They waited as stillness held, until it shifted first in the water, with small circular ripples forming where nothing touched it. The ripples overlapped in patterns too regular to belong to chance, and pressure followed in the air, felt along the skin rather than heard.

XiuQing was already moving before the disturbance fully resolved. A human and a halfling appeared in the ford as though stepping from a door already closing behind them, water sheeting around their boots where no path had been a breath before. The human knelt slowly, plunged his hand into the wet grit beneath the stone, scraped around once, twice, and came up with a mud-dark token pinched between his fingers. XiuQing landed not to meet them but to take the ground they had come through, denying the space. The man froze with the token still in his hand. Tiny rose from the near bank and called across the water, “There you are. We’ve been waiting. You’ve got the pieces, right?” He closed the distance as he spoke. The human hesitated. “Ressa?” he asked, barely moving his mouth. The halfling narrowed her eyes and answered without looking at him, “Calder, shut up. Pocket it.” The moment held long enough to feel as if it might settle before breaking cleanly.

Tiny acted first, his dart striking Calder. Panic took the human before the pain that followed; he shouted, “I’M POISONED, I’M DYING!” as he stumbled backward into the water, one hand clamped around the token as if it had become the only certain thing in the world. Ressa turned with a hand crossbow already in her hand, but Alastra’s magic arrived first, Thunderwave cracking across the ford and hurling the halfling into the far bank where she struggled slowly back to her feet. Zhale surged forward in wolf form, XiuQing closed the center between the two, and Tiny stepped up to the fallen human wielding the greatclub in both hands. Resistance collapsed in a single motion. Ressa yielded first, tight-jawed and furious beneath the calculation and the proximity of wolf teeth, and Calder yielded seconds later, still shaking and convinced he was dying. Tiny pried the Blackroot token from Calder’s grip before the man could drop it, pocket it, or swallow it. Zhale herded the halfling back across the ford to Calder’s side. Alastra approached last and said coldly, “What happens next depends entirely on how honest you decide to be.”

The two operatives flinched at the chill in her voice, so their answers came quickly, first in fragments but then revealing structure. Calder broke first because his fear had already done half the work, and Ressa corrected him only when panic made him useless. The chain revealed itself in function rather than design: no names, only movement, tokens gathered from sites after the work was complete, direction without ownership. Harlon had been expected to arrive with two tokens, one from the river and one from the ruins. Ressa and Calder had come through to retrieve the Blackroot token, collect all three, and carry the set onward. The next destination was Waterdeep, Dock Ward, Maernath Storage, in five days with someone called Red Cloak. They did not know whether Red Cloak was a person, a title, a disguise, or only the next mask in the chain. It became clear that not knowing was part of how the chain protected itself. Tiny held the three tokens for a moment, river, ruins, ford, and the pattern seemed both clearer and less useful than it should have. Then he leaned over Calder and Ressa and said, “You chose this path, unlike Merrac and Harlon, and you wielded your tiny amount of power like despots. You’d be wise if we never cross paths again.” Alastra stepped closer and said, “The people you work for will not be happy with you.” Zhale gestured south and said, “Go. And don’t stop.” The two left.

The ford did not return to what it had been. With the movement gone, what remained beneath the surface showed more clearly: a perfect circle, too precise to belong to water or time. Tiny crouched at the edge. “That’s not natural.” XiuQing studied it for a moment. “A foundation,” he said, “or a tether.” Zhale listened rather than looked. “It’s still wrong,” she said. “But not the same wrong. Those two did not do this.” They worked without separating thought from action, Zhale pressing steadying magic into the place while XiuQing eased what he could without touching what lay beneath. The surface calmed, but the depth remained unchanged until Alastra stepped forward. “It’s drawing on magic,” she said. “Let’s give it something to take.” Mage Hand formed not to grasp but to press, then vanished as the ford took it and absorbed it completely. The distortion collapsed with the spell, the water smoothed, and the pressure eased until stillness returned, not restored but emptied. Zhale then rededicated the land with an invocation to Gaia.

They stood a moment longer. “That was not normal in any way,” Alastra said. “Or natural,” Zhale replied. XiuQing stepped back. “Something is happening to the Vale.” “Fox Gap first,” Tiny said. “Yes, Mama Laran needs to hear this,” Zhale agreed. “Then Waterdeep, and Red Cloak, whatever that may be,” Alastra added. The Four turned south, and by nightfall, Gerd’s Trees rose ahead, quiet and watchful. Mama Laran opened the door before they could knock. “I wondered when you’d be back.” They gave her the shape of what had happened: the Heartstone, the tokens, the ruins, Blackroot Ford, and the way the ford had accepted and consumed Alastra’s Mage Hand as though the spell itself were something to spend. She listened without interruption.

When they finished, Mama Laran stayed quiet for a long moment, her attention not on any one of them but on the table between them, as though the pieces they had laid out there were not yet willing to make a single pattern. “I would be careful about assuming it is all one thing,” she said at last. Zhale frowned. “The river. The ford. The ruins.” “Some of that is old ground,” Mama Laran said. “Places that remember. That much I understand, or at least I understand the shape of it. But people carrying tokens from wounded places, tests done badly by frightened men, a ford twisting teleportation around itself...” She glanced toward the trees. “They may be interconnected or they may only be touching.” XiuQing inclined his head. “Then we have read it incorrectly.” “No,” she said. “You have read what was there to be read. That does not mean the page is finished.” Alastra stepped forward slightly. “The teleportation reacted to it. Badly.” Mama Laran nodded once, slowly. “Magic forced through a place already under strain can twist. That makes sense. But I would not swear yet whether what you saw was cause, symptom, or something taking advantage of both.” Silence held for a moment. “Whatever is happening there,” she finished, “it is not answering neatly to the rules I know.”

“And the Heartstone?” Zhale asked. “That,” Mama Laran said, “is quiet enough for now. Not solved. Not forgotten. But quiet enough to let you follow the warmer trail.” Tiny folded his arms. “So the tokens are one problem,” he said slowly, “or maybe one layer of one problem, and the old places are another, and whatever is underneath all of it might be something else entirely.” “Might be,” Mama Laran said. Her answer settled as direction rather than conclusion, and her gaze shifted among them. “You have three tokens now. Three sites. Three links in a chain. I would follow that while the trail is warm, but I would not let the chain convince you it is the whole creature.” Alastra nodded. “Waterdeep.” The conversation loosened into practicalities: food, the road, and the weather turning at the edge of the season. The Four stayed the night as they always did, with nothing resolved so much as carried forward, and in the morning they gathered their things and took the road north.

The road to Waterdeep gave space for thought, and thought quickly turned to concern. Tiny broke the silence first. “There’s a chance the meeting in Waterdeep is waiting for us now, not for whoever was supposed to carry the tokens.” Zhale answered at once, “Of course it is.” Alastra added, “...yes, but only if word has traveled faster than we do.” The words lingered through the day and into camp that night, not stopping them but changing the weight of every step. Zhale said again, “We should not walk into a room just because the chain points at the door.” Tiny replied, “Maybe not, but we still need to know who’s behind it.” The next day, the warning came sharper. “It will be a trap; we will be expected. They are going to try to kill us,” Zhale said. “Maybe with a room, maybe with people, maybe with a friendly face and a knife behind it, but they are going to try.” Alastra countered that abandoning the meeting would sever the chain they had spent days following, and Tiny offered what compromise he could. “Maybe we show up,” he said, then, “Maybe we don’t,” and finally, “Maybe we just watch.” No decision was made.

That second night, Tiny laid all three tokens on a flat stone near the fire and turned one beneath his fingers. “These wouldn’t allow them to be tracking us,” he said, “or we would have been found by now.” Zhale looked at the small pewter-colored lumps. “Then what are they doing?” Tiny did not answer at once. “Holding a result.” Alastra leaned closer. “Explain.” Tiny tried, haltingly at first and then with growing certainty, though certainty did not make the theory complete. The tokens did not seem to point to people. They had not called out to Merrac, Harlon, Ressa, or Calder; they had waited at sites, gathered after something had been done, then carried onward by people who knew only the next link. If the tokens were magical, and they almost certainly were, their magic felt less like a beacon and more like a sealed note that did not wish to be opened by the wrong hands. XiuQing listened without interruption, then asked, “So the chain is not a path laid for us.” Tiny shook his head once. “No. I think the path was already there. We stepped into it.” Zhale said, “Old places,” and the phrase settled between them as something half-known and half-named. Alastra concluded, “And the people moving the tokens may not know what the tokens are actually for.” Tiny gave a small confirming nod. “That would be how I’d build it.” The understanding remained incomplete but sufficient to carry forward, and the fire burned low as the conversation thinned because answers seemed likely to come only with movement. Sleep came with the quiet awareness that whatever the tokens held, someone in Waterdeep was expecting them.
