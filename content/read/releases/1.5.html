One breath held him outside the warehouse; the next placed him within it, the passage no more dramatic than a sudden pressure in the ears and a coolness across the skin, as though distance had folded around a pocket of high, pale air. Cloud Jaunt. He stood in a room that smelled of dust, old wood, and ink, crossed into the back room, found the desk, opened it without haste, and found the ledger where the message had said it would be. Names, columns, figures, notations in more than one hand, the small paper architecture by which men made dishonesty look clerical. He confirmed enough and did not indulge more. The ledger disappeared beneath his coat, and a few breaths later the cloud-jump set him outside where he had begun. He signaled, and they met at the arranged location a ways away. Zhale said, “Already?” and Alastra added, “But you were gone less than a minute.” “You didn’t really need us, did you?” XiuQing asked. Tiny turned toward home. “Let’s go,” and they withdrew together, leaving the warehouse with one absence and no story anyone could prove.

They set watches without discussion that night, the habit carrying forward even within Waterdeep’s walls because a safe house was only safe until someone proved otherwise. The city quieted by degrees around them, wheels fading, voices thinning, distant doors shutting, until three firm knocks struck the room like thrown stones. The Four moved at once. Tiny stepped to the left of the door with his back against the wall; XiuQing took the right and lifted two fingers in a silent signal; Alastra shifted back into a clean line toward the entrance; Zhale crossed to the window. The room became readiness. Tiny leaned to the slit, paused, then gave a small downward motion as recognition eased the edge without removing it. He opened the door. “Harlon?”

Harlon Lest stood in the lantern light, tired, uncertain, and plainly not far enough away. “Evening,” he said. “What are you doing here?” Tiny asked. “We told you to leave town.” Harlon shifted his weight. “...may I come in?” Tiny glanced back. “Let him in,” Alastra said, and Harlon stepped into Flat Four as though entering might be its own kind of confession. Zhale folded her arms. “You were supposed to be halfway to somewhere else by now.” “I tried.” “What happened?” Tiny asked. Harlon looked at the floor first, then at them. “I owe some people in town a little money.” Alastra asked how little, and when he said about a hundred silver, she began, “Well, that’s not much.” Zhale replied, “It is when most people make about forty gold a year.” Chagrined, Alastra conceded the point. Harlon explained that the people owed had not liked waiting and had stopped him before he left the city; that much was ordinary, miserable, and almost reassuring. The strange part came a few days after. Someone had contacted him, someone who seemed to know he had spoken with the four of them, someone who gave no name and whose appearance resisted description. “Hard to look at,” Harlon said, struggling for words. “Not invisible. Just difficult. Like they weren’t entirely there.” “Male?” Zhale asked. “Vaguely. Silvery. Sort of glowing.”

The message itself was almost offensively convenient. The unnamed contact wanted The Four to take a job. They were to go back to Fox Gap and deal with the incursions, which was precisely what they had already decided to do. If they continued that work, Harlon said, they would be considered to be working for the stranger; they did not need to answer tonight, or at all. If they went on, the stranger would know. “So we do exactly what we were already going to do,” Tiny said. “Yes.” “And that counts as their job.” “Yes.” Zhale’s eyes narrowed. “That’s convenient.” “I thought so too,” Harlon said. XiuQing asked whether Harlon himself was all right, and when Harlon said the coin they had given him had covered the debt, Zhale’s answer was immediate and simple. “Good.” He delivered no further secrets, made no demand, and seemed relieved when Tiny opened the door again. At the threshold, Harlon paused. “Take care of yourselves.” “You too,” Tiny said, and the man vanished into the quiet night. The door closed, and the room held silence for a moment before Alastra said, “...that was strange.” “Yes,” Zhale said. “Very strange,” Tiny added. XiuQing gave a small, untroubled shrug. “But efficient.”

They left Waterdeep the next morning, the twenty-eighth of Nightal, beneath a pale winter sky that made the road seem cleaner than it was. For a long while no one spoke. The city fell behind them by degrees, walls, traffic, smoke, then the broader quiet of road and field, until Tiny cleared his throat and adjusted his pack strap as if the leather had suddenly become important. “There’s something I want to tell you.” The others looked over without stopping. He kept walking. “You can start calling me Tharad.” Zhale repeated the name with faint uncertainty. “Tharad?” Alastra asked, “So that’s the name of your real cloud giant self?” He gave one measured nod. “Yes.” The word settled with more weight than any of them knew how to handle. XiuQing inclined his head and spoke it carefully, as though placing it among them with both hands. “Tharad.” The others accepted it in silence, lightly because they did not yet understand its cost, respectfully because they knew enough not to pry. None of them understood that this was the first time Tharad had ever given that name aloud to another soul.

Later that same morning, Zhale stopped so abruptly that the others turned before she finished saying, “Oh!” Brightness broke across her face. “That means we’ll be back in Fox Gap for Wintershield.” Alastra smiled at once. “That actually sounds wonderful.” XiuQing nodded. “To spend a festival with Mama Laran would be fortunate.” Tharad shrugged with the practical dignity of a man arranging his loyalties by appetite. “As long as there’s food.” “There will be,” Zhale said, laughing, and the promise warmed the road ahead for a while. Late the next day, that warmth met the usual reality of travel. Six figures stepped out of the trees ahead, two more behind, their spacing and stillness better than ordinary roadside thieves and worse for that. One began, “Drop your packs and...” but The Four were already moving. Tharad stepped forward, XiuQing flowed into the opening, Zhale shifted her stance, and Alastra retreated just enough to give herself a line and them room. Steel rang once, then again, before Alastra’s voice cracked through the trees in furious Draconic, carrying weight beyond vocabulary. The fight died standing. Their leader lowered his sword. “...we’ll go.” Tharad shook his head. “Drop the weapons.” A breath of hesitation survived until Alastra took one step forward, and then swords, knives, and clubs clattered to the road in a sudden metallic rain. Zhale said, “Now leave,” and they did.

The abandoned weapons looked both ridiculous and dangerous in the mud. Zhale gestured at the pile with weary indignation. “Great. What the hell are we supposed to do with all of these?” Tharad crouched and began gathering them. “We carry them down the road a bit.” Alastra watched. “And then?” “We bury them.” “...seriously?” Zhale asked. Tharad shrugged. “We did it before, what’s the problem? If we leave them here, the bandits come back and pick them up.” The logic was ugly and therefore impossible to dispute. They dug a shallow pit beside the road, buried the entire bundle, smoothed over the disturbed earth as best they could, and left it there, a small civic improvement no bard would ever sing. Zhale patted the ground flat with one dry, “Well, that was overkill.” They pressed on until night deepened, and late on the thirtieth of Nightal the lights of Fox Gap appeared through the trees, warm and steady against the cold dark. Zhale’s expression softened. “Home,” she said, and the word carried more shelter than the lights themselves.

Fox Gap lay beneath frost on Wintershield morning, the first of Hammer in the year 1501 DR, holding itself in that new year festival stillness where no work had yet been done and even the smoke from the chimney seemed to climb more gently. Inside Mama Laran’s cottage, The Four gathered around a table scattered with maps, warming their hands on steaming cider and planning the day: Silent Patch first, Quiet Lake second, with a stop at Naiad’s Bend to check the old wound in the river. Alastra stepped in and the room fell silent, not from danger this time but because her hair had remade itself overnight into three long braids, each falling from the back of her head in a different hue that seemed to have negotiated separately with nature. Mama Laran blinked. “...is that your Wintershield?” Alastra frowned. “My what?” “Your hair.” Alastra drew one braid forward, examined it as though it belonged to someone who owed her an explanation, and sighed. “I have no control over it.” Tharad leaned back. “You mean it just does whatever it wants?” “Yes.” XiuQing asked, “Every day?” “More or less.” Zhale leaned forward, incredulous. “So your hair is just... random.” “Completely.” Tharad considered this with sober sympathy. “Well. That just seems annoying.” It was not a solution, but it was accurate enough that Alastra did not argue.

The hair passed into the morning’s ordinary catalogue of strangeness, and Alastra took her seat with a gesture toward the map. “So, which disaster are we fixing first?” Mama Laran, watching them with quiet satisfaction, reminded them that if they passed back by near midday they should stop for lunch. Alastra brightened at once. “You’re cooking?” “Of course I’m cooking,” Mama Laran said, and promised hot buttered ale. Tharad straightened as if a commander had finally named an objective worth bloodshed. “Now that sounds like Wintershield.” Zhale grinned. “Oh gods, I haven’t had hot buttered ale since last winter.” Nutbread followed in the promise, and Tharad clapped once. “Alright, now we’ve got something to work towards.” XiuQing inclined his head. “I look forward to experiencing this tradition.” As they gathered their things, Mama Laran’s tone shifted. “Oh, and be careful out there.” They turned back. “There’s been something big rooting around in the woods. Villagers have been talking about it for a few days now.” Zhale frowned. “What kind of big?” Mama Laran shrugged lightly. “I haven’t gone looking yet. And I’m certainly not doing it today. It’s Wintershield.” “Fair,” Tharad said. “Just keep your eyes open. Something’s been knocking trees over.” Alastra raised an eyebrow. “That does sound big.” Mama Laran smiled faintly and sent them out into the frost with a cook’s final authority. “Come back for lunch and tell me what you found.”

They had not gone far before Zhale halted. “Campfire.” The smell led them to a rough campsite where embers still glowed beneath a poor scattering of ash, and she smothered them with dirt and water while muttering, “I cannot believe they left this burning.” Tharad crouched nearby, reading ground rather than flame. “Someone didn’t leave.” A few moments later they found him: a muscular man tied shirtless to a tree, wrists bound tight behind the trunk, posture stiff with cold and pain but expression almost comically composed. “Morning,” he said. Alastra stared. “...hello?” “What are you doing here?” Tharad asked. The man shrugged as much as the ropes allowed. “Well, I was traveling with a mercenary group.” Zhale was already cutting him free. “Which one?” “The Iron Pike Company.” As the ropes loosened, he added, as though explaining a delay in delivery, “They decided I wasn’t worth wasting healing on.” Alastra winced. When Zhale had him free, she asked, “They left you tied to a tree?” He stretched his arms carefully. “Yep. They said if I lived, I could untie myself eventually.” Tharad studied him then, the strength, the grounded way he moved, the plainness that did not read as stupidity so much as an absence of needless ornament. “What’s your name?” “Veric.” “Tiny,” Tharad said automatically, then gestured to the others. “Zhale. Alastra. XiuQing.” Veric nodded to each in turn. He asked if he could come along, and when Alastra pointed out that he was not exactly equipped for travel, he looked around and then admitted, “...fair point.”

Tharad unbuckled his greatclub and offered it without ceremony. “Here.” Veric took it, and Tharad drew his rapier with the ease of someone returning to the tool that actually belonged to his hand. “I’ll use this instead.” Veric noted the motion with quiet approval. “You’re good with that.” “Better than the club.” Zhale told him they were going to a place called Silent Patch, and when Veric asked what was there, The Four exchanged a glance already familiar with the limits of honest answers. Alastra gave him a small smile. “We’re not entirely sure.” Tharad tossed him a shirt. Veric caught it a fraction late, almost dropped it, and then shrugged off the clumsiness. “Still waking up.” “Let’s go,” Tharad said, and Veric came along as if that were the obvious next step in a morning that had begun tied to a tree.

Silent Patch announced itself not with sight but with subtraction. Sound thinned before the clearing fully opened, footsteps softening too quickly, breath seeming to fall away from itself, the forest holding still with the exhausted obedience of a place that had been told for generations not to speak. Zhale slowed. “This is it.” Tharad snapped his fingers and listened to the little deadened click. “...huh.” Mama Laran’s friends, the Hedge Druids of Waterdeep’s Environs, who expressly did not refer to themselves as a Circle, had explained that this place too had been wrong for about two and a half hundred years, that animals avoided it and sound faded there. Veric, who had joined them less than an hour earlier and had no reason yet to pretend calm expertise, listened and said, “I can hear that.” XiuQing found the center by stillness rather than mark, guiding them to a flattened circle hidden beneath grass. “Here.” Zhale recognized what the place had once held. “That’s one of them.” “Yep,” Tharad explained to Veric, “where something like an anchor had been.” Alastra looked over the depression. “So whatever sat here was heavy... but not solid.” “Or not solid all the time,” Tharad added. Veric asked, “...then why isn’t the grass dead or flattened?” “Because it was never really here?” Zhale suggested. “Well. Let’s see if we can help,” Tharad said. They tried what had worked before: healing, Spare the Dying, Alastra searching and finding only a hum that barely shifted. “I was expecting a lattice,” she said, and there was enough irritation in it to mean she had expected the world to be logical.

Then Veric began to hum. It was not an idea at first, only a sound made by a large man with no training in caution and no habit of disguising what passed through him. “Mhmhmhmhm...” The clearing answered. All four turned. “...what?” he asked. Tharad said, “Do that again.” Veric hummed again, uncertain but willing, and the response deepened. XiuQing took out his flute and joined with one clean note, then another, and said calmly, “Help me.” Tharad added the bright ring of crowbars struck together, surprise flickering across his face at the usefulness of such a ridiculous instrument, while Zhale brought a steady rhythm beneath them, shield and the butt end of her sickle pommel creating a pulse the deadened clearing could follow. Each layer weakened the wrongness until all eyes turned to Alastra. She sighed. “...fine. I hate singing,” and then her voice rose into the harmony with such effortless precision that for one perfect beat everyone stopped making sound at all. XiuQing’s flute faltered. Tharad froze with the crowbars half-raised. Zhale lowered her shield. Alastra blinked. “What?” “Since when can you do that?” Tharad asked. “Private lessons. My mother made me practice for years.” The music resumed around her, now steadier for knowing its own shape, and Veric observed, “You’re very good.” The disturbance collapsed inward by degrees, not shattered but settled, and when the clearing finally went still in the right way, XiuQing sealed the work with Spare the Dying and Zhale followed with her ritual to Gaia, while the others stood in quiet reverence. Veric watched, untroubled. When it was done, he said, “Well. That was weird,” then lifted one hand before anyone could explain. “No, I get it. The music calmed the energy that was there. Stabilized it. Then you healed it.” Alastra looked at him for a moment. “That is exactly what we did.” Veric nodded. “Makes sense.”

The proof came after: a bird flew straight across the open space where earlier every bird had bent around the clearing as though avoiding an unseen wall, and a moment later crickets returned from the brush, faint at first, then ordinary. Zhale heard them and nodded. “Good.” They continued toward Naiad’s Bend with the lightness that sometimes follows work finished cleanly. When they reached the river, they found it calm, stable, and unchanged, its earlier wound quiet enough to let the day be a festival again. Essaerae rose from the water in a shimmer of blue and spray, and Veric shouted, “OH!” with such unguarded delight that he rushed forward with his arms open wide before the naiad’s recoil brought him instantly to a halt. “Oh,” he said softly, stepping back. Then, without embarrassment, he beckoned gently, almost childlike, asking without words for permission to play. Essaerae studied him, wary and curious, then yielded. Laughter followed, then spinning, then ring-around-the-rosy in the winter river’s shallows. The Four watched in silence while something in the day eased around them.

When the game ended, and Veric stood happily beside them again, they spoke with Essaerae about the river and the work still unfolding across the Vale. The naiad listened with the grave attention of water that remembered more than it explained, and at last she said, “You are doing more than healing. You are warding the land against what comes.” Tharad reacted, took the word warding silently and held it. Alastra invited Essaerae to lunch, because Alastra’s manners could step over almost any boundary and offer hospitality anyway, but Essaerae declined gently. “I cannot leave my river just yet. But Happy Wintershield to you.” Zhale returned the greeting, and they left the bank with the river behind them running like itself. On the walk back, Tharad slowed. “Wait.” The others looked over. “What Essaerae said earlier. The word warding.” He walked a few more paces, letting the thought land properly. “We’ve been calling ourselves The Four. What if we called ourselves the Wardens?” The name settled among them with the immediate rightness of a key turned in the proper lock. Zhale answered first. “...yes.” Alastra smiled. “Oh, that’s perfect.” XiuQing inclined his head. “It is accurate.” Tharad said, with a certainty that made the matter complete, “Good. We’re the Wardens.” From a few paces behind, Veric considered the new word, nodded once, and said, “Hm. Well, the name suits you.” Tharad glanced back. “Us.”
